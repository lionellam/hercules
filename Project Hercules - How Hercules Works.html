PROJECT HERCULES · READABLE EDITION

How Hercules works

Hercules is a private expense tracker for family and close friends. You type an expense the way you'd say it, Claude turns it into a tidy record, and you check it before it's saved.

Based on: Specification v0.28 and Architecture v0.8 Status: live on Vercel since 1 November 2026 Owner: Lionel Lam

This page explains the two technical documents in plain language, with mockups of the screens. The original documents remain the source of truth. Where this page simplifies, they have the full detail.

Part 1 · The app (from the Specification)

The four principles

Every feature is checked against these.

You always check first

Nothing Claude reads from your text is saved until you've seen it and had the chance to fix it.

AI only where it helps

Claude only reads your words and suggests the fields. Saving, checking, totals and everything else is ordinary, predictable code.

Your words are kept

What you typed is stored alongside the record, so parsing mistakes can be looked into later.

Private by account

Several people use one app, but nobody can see anyone else's expenses.

Who does what

WhoTheir part
YouSign in with Google, type an expense, check what Claude found, fix anything, save.
Claude (Haiku 5.5)Reads your text and suggests the amount, category, merchant and date. That's all.
The appEverything else: sign-in and the invite list, checking the fields, storing, totals, budgets and the daily usage limit.

An earlier version runs entirely on a home computer (a command-line tool using a local model) and is kept separately for experiments.

Logging an expense

The main flow, start to finish. The highlighted step is the only one involving AI.

  1. You type it in plain words, for example "Lunch at Ya Kun for $4.50". The heading, the box and today's running total stay pinned at the top of the screen, so you can log from anywhere in the list.
  2. The app gathers your categories and passes the list to Claude, so it can only choose from your own.
  3. Claude reads the text and returns the amount, category, merchant and date in a fixed format.AI It must pick one of your categories, or "Other" if nothing fits. It can't invent new ones.
  4. The app matches the category by its exact name. No guessing.
  5. You see a review card with every field editable.
  6. You fix anything that's wrong by editing the fields directly. Claude isn't asked again.
  7. You save. The record is stored with your original words and a note of whether you changed anything, which shows how accurate the parsing is over time.
  8. It appears everywhere: in Today, History and Summary. Tap any saved expense to edit or delete it. Deleting asks first, and the record can still be recovered.

The other screens

There are four tabs: Log, History, Summary and Categories. On every one, the heading and its main control stay pinned at the top while the rest scrolls. A sun or moon icon switches between light and dark mode. On a tablet or computer, the app widens to two columns, and uses more of the window on larger screens.

Summary

Summary shows the current month and the one before. For each category it shows the amount spent and a bar. If you've set a budget, the bar fills against that budget, turning red once you're over. A pie chart shows each category's share, with at most six slices. Categories with nothing spent that month aren't listed. For the current month only, budgeted categories also show your spending pace.

Spending pace, explained

Pace answers one question: am I spending faster than my budget allows? It only applies to the current month, and only to categories that have a budget and some spending.

First, the app works out how much of the budget you'd have used by today if you spent it evenly through the month. Today counts as a full day, so on the last day of the month the expected amount equals the whole budget.

expected by today = budget × today's day of the month ÷ days in the month

Then it picks the note under the bar. The rules are checked in order, and the first one that matches wins.

CheckIf…The note says
1stYou've spent more than the whole budgetOver budget by $X, in red with a warning icon
2ndYou've spent exactly the whole budgetBudget used · $0.00 left
3rdSpent is within 5% of the full monthly budget either side of expectedOn pace
4thSpent is above that range$X over pace (spent − expected)
5thSpent is below that range$X under pace (expected − spent)

For the 3rd to 5th notes, the app adds what's left and roughly how much you can spend a day: "$Y left, about $Z/day". Z is what's left divided by the days remaining after today. On the last day it reads "$Y left today" instead.

Why rent says "Budget used", not "over pace". Paying the whole $2,000 on day 1 is early, not overspending. Because the "Budget used" check comes before the pace checks, that's what rent shows. For the same reason, red is used only for going over budget. Being over pace stays in the normal colour.

Reading the burn-up chart. Tap a budgeted category to open its chart. The solid line is your running total, which steps up on each day you spent. The dashed line is the budget spread evenly over the month, and the upright line marks today. Above the dashed line means over pace. Point at or drag across the chart to see any day, and the "Daily figures" table underneath lists the same numbers.

Things it deliberately doesn't do.

  • No month-end forecast. Projecting from spending so far is badly wrong early in the month or after a one-off purchase. A $640 purchase on day 3 would "forecast" $6,400.
  • No settling-in period. Early in the month the expected amounts are small, so a single coffee can show as over pace. This was chosen to keep things simple.
  • No budget history. Only the current budget is stored, so earlier months aren't compared against a budget.

What's stored

Three kinds of record, each belonging to one person's account.

RecordWhat it holdsWorth knowing
ExpenseAmount, currency, category, merchant, date, remarks, and the words you originally typedNotes whether you edited Claude's suggestion. Deleting hides the record but keeps it recoverable. Imported records note where they came from, so the import is safe to run twice.
CategoryName, an optional monthly budget, and flags for "Other" and for retired categoriesEveryone starts with 14 categories (Food & Dining, Transport, Groceries, Entertainment, Utilities, Health & Medical, Household, Shopping, Rent, Clothes, Education, Professional Development, Travel and Other). Names must be unique regardless of capitals, up to 50 per person.
UserGoogle account, display name, and whether the account is activeCreated and given the starter categories the first time an invited person signs in.

Amounts are in Singapore dollars. An expense can record another currency, but totals add the numbers as they are without converting. Each person can parse up to 100 expenses a day, which keeps model costs in check.

What Claude does, and doesn't

Claude does

  • Read your text and suggest the amount, merchant and date
  • Pick the best category from your own list, or "Other"

The app does, never Claude

  • Check what Claude returned
  • Fetch your categories and match the chosen one
  • Save, find and edit records
  • Manage categories and budgets
  • Sign-in, the invite list and usage limits

Keeping this line clear keeps the AI part small, cheap and easy to check, and stops the model creeping into jobs that ordinary code does better.

Open questions and not yet in scope

Still to decide

  • Several items at once. Should "coffee $4 and lunch $12" become one record or two?
  • Uncertain fields. Should the review card flag fields Claude had to guess, such as a date you didn't mention?
  • Storing the original words. Keep them in the same record, as now, or in a separate audit table?
  • Which fields were edited. Record which fields you changed, not just whether you changed any.

Described but not built yet

  • Merchant suggestions from your past entries. The merchant field is plain text for now.
  • A sub-label for "Other", such as "gift" or "charity". The database has room for it, but the review card doesn't ask for it yet.

Not in this version

  • Budget alerts, budget history and rollover
  • Searching and exporting
  • Sharing between accounts
  • Receipt photos and bank imports
  • Logging while offline (the app keeps what you've typed until you're back online)
  • Currency conversion
  • Deleting or retiring categories in the app
  • Managing invitations in the app

Part 2 · How it's built (from the Architecture)

The system

One web app, hosted on Vercel in Singapore, talks to three outside services. Your phone runs it like an app from the home screen.

Key decisions

Each decision keeps the number it has in the Architecture document. Numbers are in the order decisions were made, so they don't run in sequence within each group.

Who can use it

#1

Family and close friends only

It meets a personal and family need. It isn't a public product.

#2

An invite list of email addresses

Anyone can try to sign in with Google, but only invited addresses get in. The list is re-checked on every request, so removing someone takes effect at once.

#3

Google sign-in only

Free, familiar, and works on iPhone and Android. Sign in with Apple would need a US$99-a-year developer membership.

Claude and privacy

#4

Expense text is sent to a cloud AI provider

Only to a provider that doesn't train on API data. This allows a hosted app with no computer running at home, a trade-off against the earlier "everything local" approach.

#5

Claude Haiku 5.5, with a fixed answer format

Fast and cheap, and simple extraction is well within its abilities. The fixed format means replies always come back in the expected shape. Gemini's free tier was turned down because its terms allow free-tier inputs to be used to improve Google's products.

#6

Cloud model only for the web app

A hosted app can't reach a model running on a home computer.

#7

Two cost guards

A US$5 monthly spend limit on the Anthropic account, and 100 parses per person per day. Normal use costs cents a month; the limits protect against bugs or misuse.

Hosting and data

#8

Hosted on Vercel's free Hobby plan

A natural fit for this kind of app. The plan's terms were checked and suit a private, non-commercial family app.

#9

A free vercel.app web address, for now

No cost, and Google sign-in works with it. A custom domain can be added later without code changes.

#10

Neon for the database

Standard Postgres built for this kind of hosting, with a free tier. A simple file database can't persist on Vercel.

#11

A one-off import of the old data

229 records from July 2026 onwards were brought across, so the full history is in one place.

#12

The old command-line tool stays separate

Kept as an offline tool for experiments. Anything logged there after the import isn't synced.

#22

The server runs in Singapore

Next to the database and the people using it. The default US location sent every request across the Pacific and back. Unauthenticated test requests went from 1.1–1.5 seconds to 0.06–0.88 seconds after the move.

How the app behaves

#13

One language, front to back

TypeScript with Next.js for both the screens and the server. The parsing prompt was carried over from the earlier Python version.

#14

Installs from the browser

Add it to the home screen and it opens full-screen like an app, with no App Store needed.

#15

No offline logging

Parsing needs the internet. Offline, the app says so and keeps what you've typed.

#23

Data kept in memory for instant tabs

Screens show what was last loaded straight away and refresh quietly in the background. Any save, edit or delete reloads everything. Nothing is stored on the phone, so it's gone when the app closes.

#24

Moderately wider on bigger screens

Phone layout up to 767 pixels wide. From 768 pixels the app widens to 820 pixels, then to 920 pixels on an iPad held sideways or a small laptop and 1,000 pixels on a larger laptop or desktop, with two columns on Log and Summary and edit forms as centred dialogs. The bottom tab bar stays at every size.

#25

Light and dark mode

Follows the device until you tap the toggle, then remembers your choice on that device. It's a display preference, so it isn't stored in your account.

Scope

#16

Started with four screens

Sign-in, Log, History and Summary, to get a working app into use quickly. Categories came later (#26).

#17

Categories edited directly in the database

Replaced by #26. The database was designed per person from the start, so adding a screen needed no restructuring.

#18

Edit and delete by reusing the review card

No extra screen needed. Deleted records stay recoverable.

#19

Singapore dollars only

Nearly every expense is in SGD. The currency is still recorded, but totals don't convert.

#26

A Categories tab to add and rename

Expenses link to categories by ID, so a rename carries through the whole history, and Claude reads the up-to-date list. Retiring categories is left for later.

#27

An optional monthly budget per category

The simplest start on budgets. Only the current amount is kept; a fuller budget record can replace it if history is ever needed.

#28

Spending pace on Summary

Answers "am I spending too fast?" without a new screen. Worked out on your phone from data already loaded, so no server or database changes were needed. See Spending pace.
